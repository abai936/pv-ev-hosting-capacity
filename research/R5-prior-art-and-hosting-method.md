## Summary

- The space is already occupied by very recent work. `mhasan-ee/ieee34-hosting-capacity` (created 2026-11-16) does IEEE 34-bus, 8,760 h time series, PV sizing sweep, volt-var, validation within 0.14% of IEEE published results, fault study. `mohammed-abouzeid7/ieee13-feeder-study` (created 2026-11-10) does IEEE 13 load flow, fault and load-hosting sweep. "Validated against IEEE published voltages" is therefore no longer a differentiator on its own.
- No repo I read combines PV + EV + smart-inverter control + unbalance reporting on IEEE 13 or 34, with tests. The one that has PV + EV + volt-var + Monte Carlo (`SJW112/pv-ev-hosting-capacity`) uses a Melbourne-tutorial MV-LV network, shows no validation and no tests, and has 2 commits.
- Across all 10 repos read in detail: none shows an automated test suite that passes (DREAMS has a `tests/` folder, contents not shown; opendss-mcp-server has 16 test files but its README gives two conflicting coverage figures). None shows CI status. Half show no licence.
- The best README practice seen is `gopeshgill/ev-hosting-capacity` (IEEE 123, EV + BESS): headline result first, validation table, and an assumptions table stating which way each assumption biases the result. Copy the idea, not the code.
- Method sources agree on the core definition: hosting capacity is the DER amount a feeder can accept before a technical limit is violated under existing controls and infrastructure (EPRI 2015; RACE for 2030 2021). It is not one number: it depends on location, load, controls, and which criteria are included.
- NZ / AS-NZS content: I found no NZ-specific hosting-capacity source in this pass. NZ settings (voltage limits, AS/NZS 4777.2 inverter settings) are unverified and must be sourced separately before they go into the project.

## Differentiators for a new project (10)

Each is a gap seen above. "Seen" lists what the reviewed repos did or did not show.

1. **Written definition and criteria table at the top of the README.** State the hosting-capacity definition used (the EPRI / RACE style definition), the exact criteria and thresholds, and report the *binding constraint* for every result. Seen: most READMEs give a number without a stated definition; mhasan-ee does name the limiting bus.
2. **Hand-calculation validation as well as IEEE-benchmark validation.** Check OpenDSS against a simple closed-form case (for example single-feeder voltage rise, roughly (R·P + X·Q)/V², and a two-bus loss calculation) and show the delta. Also keep the IEEE published-voltage comparison. Seen: two repos compare to IEEE published results; none shows a hand calculation.
3. **Real automated tests plus CI.** `pytest` for model load, convergence, power balance, limit logic and a known-answer hosting-capacity case, run by GitHub Actions with a visible badge. Seen: no repo shows a passing test suite with CI; the one with tests has contradictory coverage claims.
4. **Reproducibility.** Pinned `requirements.txt` and Python version, fixed random seeds, one-command run (for example a `make` target or single script), results CSVs and figure scripts committed, OpenDSSDirect.py rather than Windows-only COM. Add `CITATION.cff` and a tagged release. Seen: only mhasan-ee and gopeshgill give full repro steps.
5. **A licence.** MIT or BSD-3. Seen: 5 of the 10 show no licence.
6. **PV and EV together on the same unbalanced feeder, in time series.** Show coincidence: midday PV export against evening EV charging, single-phase placement per phase. Seen: the only PV + EV repo uses a different network and has no validation.
7. **Voltage unbalance (and per-phase results) reported explicitly.** EPRI 2015 says unbalance and thermal limits are usually *not* the binding PV constraint but can be with clustered or single-phase PV; showing your own data on that is a credible engineering result. Seen: unbalance is "if modelled" in one repo and absent elsewhere.
8. **Control modes compared on the same case:** unity PF, fixed PF, volt-var, volt-watt, with curtailed energy (kWh) reported as the cost of the extra hosting capacity. Seen: volt-var in one repo, volt-watt in another, never compared with curtailment cost.
9. **Method comparison on one feeder:** worst-case snapshot vs snapshot sweep vs time series vs Monte Carlo (P5 / P50 / P95 hosting capacity from random placement with fixed seeds). Showing how the method choice moves the answer is the clearest "I understand the method" signal. Seen: no repo compares methods.
10. **Honest limitations and an assumptions table with bias direction** (as gopeshgill does), plus sensitivity runs (load level, source voltage, regulator settings). State plainly: simulation only, IEEE feeders are US 60 Hz test models, no field data, protection not assessed unless you actually do it.
11. **(Optional, needs sourcing first) NZ / AS-NZS framing.** Rescale or re-label voltage limits and inverter settings to NZ practice, and say which settings are US defaults and which are NZ. Candidate items to look up and cite before use (not read in this pass): NZ statutory voltage limits at the point of supply, AS/NZS 4777.2 volt-var and volt-watt settings, and a distributor's published small-scale DG connection standard. Do not state any NZ number in the README until it is cited.

### Plain-English comparison of the four approaches (second-year level)

A *worst-case deterministic* study picks the scariest single situation (for example, midday sun and minimum load) and asks how much PV the feeder can take before a limit, such as voltage above the allowed maximum, is crossed; it is quick and safe but usually too pessimistic because that situation rarely happens everywhere at once. A *snapshot sweep* repeats that idea over many chosen cases (different PV sizes, locations, load levels), each a single power flow with no sense of time, so you see how the answer changes but not how often a problem occurs. A *time-series* study runs power flows through a whole day or year of real load and sun profiles, so it shows when and for how many hours limits are broken, and it captures things like EV charging in the evening and inverter controls reacting to voltage, at the price of more computing and needing good profile data. A *stochastic* (Monte Carlo) study runs hundreds of random draws of where the PV and EVs are placed, how big they are, and what the load is, and reports a distribution (for example, the median and a 5th-percentile "safe" value) instead of a single number; it is the most honest about uncertainty but the slowest and the hardest to explain. Sources 1 to 3 support the criteria and the general trend from deterministic to stochastic; the plain-language comparison itself is my synthesis, not a quote from any of them (INFERRED).

### Criteria as supported by the sources read

- Overvoltage and voltage deviation: EPRI 2015 (VERIFIED). Thresholds used in the repos read: 0.95-1.05 pu (SJW112 repo); 0.9-1.1 pu for an LV study (ref 3).
- Thermal overload (line and transformer current, ≤100% in SJW112 repo): ref 3 and EPRI 2015 (VERIFIED).
- Voltage unbalance: 2% VUF limit in ref 3, citing EN 50160; a 3% figure appeared in a search snippet attributed to an NREL table, not opened (INFERRED).
- Reverse power flow: EPRI 2015 mentions reverse-flow overload; a Wollongong review snippet linked it to overvoltage and unbalance (INFERRED, not opened).
- Protection: EPRI 2015 (VERIFIED). This needs a short-circuit study with inverter fault contribution, which is beyond a pure power-flow sweep.
