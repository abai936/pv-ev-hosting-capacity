PV + EV hosting capacity, OpenDSS and Python

Can an NZ street take more rooftop solar and EV chargers?

  1. I simulated one 55-house low-voltage street in OpenDSS and Python.
  2. I asked how much solar each house can export, and how many EV chargers can run at once.
  3. The cable heating limit hits before voltage does.
  4. Everything here is simulated, with synthetic data.

The feeder has 55 customers and 907 buses. The NZ low-voltage limit used is 207 to 253 V (230 V, plus or minus 10%, since 13 Nov 2025). Sources: README.md and research/R3-nz-standards-and-limits.md.

Words used on this page

  • Hosting capacity: the most solar (in kW) each solar house can export before something breaks a limit.
  • pu: per unit. Here 1 pu is 230 V.
  • Customer-hours: hours counted for each customer, added up. Two customers over the limit for one hour is two customer-hours.
  • Binding limit: the limit that is hit first, so it sets the answer.

NZ context

NZ lines companies are setting solar export limits right now.

  1. On 7 Apr 2026 the Electricity Authority decided a default static export limit of 10 kW for small-scale solar, and a standard export limit assessment method.
  2. Dates from the EA information sheet: 11 May 2026 (10 kW default), 11 Sep 2026 ("Australia A" inverter settings for all new low-voltage generation applications), 11 Nov 2026 (assessment methods required).
  3. Orion publishes how it works out hosting capacity: a load-flow model of each LV network, with export raised "in increments of 1 kW per phase" until a voltage or thermal limit or a 10 kW per phase cap.
  4. This project uses the same kind of question, per phase, and the Australia A settings. It is similar in shape, not identical, and it does not implement the EA's new assessment method.

Per connection (EA) is not the same as per phase (this project and Orion). Do not compare the numbers. The EA sheet also says studies "may only consider current hosting capacity", so forecasts are not part of today's rules.

Trade-off and failure card

Inverter settings fix voltage, but not the cable limit.

  1. Australia A volt-var and volt-watt settings cut the year's time above 253 V from 1,330 to 38.5 customer-hours.
  2. They do not raise the snapshot hosting capacity: 9.3 / 4.9 / 2.7 kW with control, 9.5 / 5.0 / 2.7 kW without.
  3. The cost is more current in the cables: hours with any line above its assumed rating go from 326.5 to 582.5, and the transformer peak from 195.1 to 216.6 kVA.
  4. A 2.5% lower transformer tap helps clustered solar (5.4 to 7.5 kW at 30%) but cuts the lowest evening voltage with no EVs from 233.25 V to 227.08 V.

Failure story: a bug found in review

A bug was found: OpenDSS inverter control keeps its previous state, so 730 of 1,272 hosting-capacity values depended on the order they were solved in. The largest shift was 1.07 kW. A cold-start step and a regression test fixed it.

An independent review step found it; the fix was written with an AI coding assistant. Sources: CP-08-stage8-mitigation.md, tests/test_stage8.py, VALIDATION.md row 28.

How the model was checked

What I checked, and what I did not.

  1. The 907-bus feeder matches the published OpenDSS solution to 0.003 V (worst case, with constant-power loads) and the GridLAB-D one to 0.001 V.
  2. Toy circuits match hand phasor calculations to 4 decimals.
  3. One hand check: 5 kW at the far-end house raises the voltage by 4.787 V by hand and 4.787 V in OpenDSS.
  4. Not checked: PV, EV, inverter and rating results have no independent source, and the energy balances only prove signs and units.

EV result as a range

How many EV chargers at once? Two to six of 55 houses, depending on the evening.

  1. On a typical winter weekday evening, 6 of 55 houses can charge a 7.4 kW EV at the same time with balanced placement.
  2. On one busy evening it is 2 of 55 (3.6%).
  3. With random placement the count is 2 / 5 / 7 (5th, 50th, 95th percentile of 50 orders).
  4. The binding limit is the assumed trunk cable rating; voltage never binds before 30 EVs. A 5% rating change moved the count from 6 to 9.

A whole year

Over a year, 8 kWp per house is a lot more than 5 kWp.

  1. 17,520 half-hour steps for 2022, with 60% of houses on solar.
  2. 5 kWp per house gives 4.5 customer-hours above 253 V; 8 kWp gives 1,330.
  3. Even with no solar, a cable rating (mostly the trunk) is exceeded for 141.5 hours a year.
  4. So thermal results are relative, not absolute.

Limits

What this cannot tell you.

  1. Simulation only; no measured data; no real feeder compared.
  2. Cable ratings are assumed. A 5% change moved the EV count from 6 to 9.
  3. Synthetic load: every house is statistically identical, which tends to make results look better than reality.
  4. 30-minute steps hide short peaks.
  5. One feeder: nothing certain about other networks.

Source: LIMITATIONS.md items 1, 3, 4, 6, 9. The full list has 16 items, most with a bias direction.

Forecast, not prediction

What if this street looked like NZ in 2027?

Forecast, not prediction. These are modelled forecasts from national data (EA solar, Ministry of Transport EVs via Figure.NZ). They are not measurements and not predictions for this feeder.

  1. A simple "last year's trend" model beat the S-curve and driver models in the back-test (mean absolute error in percentage points of share: solar 0.13, EV 0.92).
  2. The 80% ranges were too narrow: they held the truth only 40% (solar) and 27% (EV) of the time.
  3. Solar share of houses, low / mid / high, Dec 2027: 5.0 / 5.1 / 5.6%. A system the size of today's new ones first exceeds the simulated limit at about 34% penetration. Caveat: the hosting capacity is kW per single-phase solar customer, while a typical new system is whole-house size, so this is a rough comparison, not like for like.

The Electricity Authority says studies may only consider current hosting capacity, and it will consider forecast uptake in a future policy consultation. This back-test is a small step in that direction. My forecast is national solar and EV share, not low-voltage DG uptake, and its ranges were too narrow.
